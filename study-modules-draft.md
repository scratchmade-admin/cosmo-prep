# Study Modules Draft

## Module 1: Infection Control and Sanitation

### Overview
This is one of the highest-value areas on the written exam because it affects every service. When in doubt, the safest answer usually wins. Focus on preventing cross-contamination, knowing what can and cannot be disinfected, and following blood exposure procedure correctly.

### Key concepts
- **Cleaning** removes visible dirt and debris.
- **Disinfection** kills most harmful microorganisms on nonporous tools and surfaces.
- **Sterilization** is the highest level and is not the standard answer for most everyday salon tools unless the question specifically points there.
- **Porous items** cannot be properly disinfected once contaminated. Many must be thrown away.
- **Nonporous items** can usually be cleaned and then disinfected.
- **Single-use items** are used once and discarded immediately after use.
- **Universal precautions** mean treating blood and body fluids as potentially infectious.
- **Handwashing, gloves, and clean workstation setup** are basic infection-control habits, not optional extras.
- For **blood exposure**, stop service, glove up, control the exposure, clean and disinfect the area, bag contaminated waste as required, then wash hands.

### Common exam traps
- Mixing up **cleaning** and **disinfection**.
- Choosing to disinfect something that is **porous or single-use** instead of discarding it.
- Forgetting that **hands must be washed before and after** a service when appropriate.
- Picking the “fast” answer instead of the **safest** answer after a cut or blood exposure.
- Assuming a tool is safe just because it “looks clean.”

### Quick memory cues
- **See dirt? Clean first.**
- **Hard surface = maybe disinfect. Soft/porous = probably toss.**
- **Blood changes everything. Stop, protect, clean, disinfect.**
- **If it touches more than one client, sanitation rules follow it.**

### Self-check
- What is the difference between cleaning and disinfection?
- Which items are usually discarded instead of disinfected?
- What is the first priority if blood is present during a service?
- Why are nonporous tools treated differently from porous ones?

## Module 2: California Law and Professional Safety

### Overview
This module is about choosing the legal, safe, and professional action. The exam often tests whether you can recognize when a service should not be performed, what falls inside scope of practice, and what protects the client best.

### Key concepts
- **Scope of practice** means doing only services your license allows.
- A license must be **current, valid, and properly displayed** as required.
- **Refuse service** when a condition is unsafe, contagious, outside scope, or likely to harm the client.
- **Consumer protection** means honest, sanitary, safe service and proper professional behavior.
- **Contraindications** are signs or conditions that make a service unsafe or inappropriate.
- Professional safety includes clean work habits, chemical caution, and not guessing beyond your training.

### Common exam traps
- Choosing a helpful-sounding answer that is **outside legal scope**.
- Continuing a service when the question clearly shows a **contraindication**.
- Assuming you should diagnose a disorder instead of **referring the client appropriately**.
- Confusing “customer service” with “do whatever the client wants.” Safety and law come first.

### Quick memory cues
- **If it is unsafe, illegal, or out of scope, do not do it.**
- **Nice is good. Safe is required.**
- **You are licensed to perform services, not diagnose medical conditions.**
- **When unsure, protect the client and stay inside scope.**

### Self-check
- What should you do if a client requests a service outside your license scope?
- When should a service be refused or delayed?
- Why is recognizing contraindications so important on the exam?
- What matters more on state board questions: pleasing the client or following safe legal practice?

## Module 3: Hair and Scalp Care

### Overview
This section covers the basic science behind hair services. You do not need a chemistry degree. You do need to know the structure of hair, how it grows, what common scalp conditions look like, and how pH, porosity, and elasticity affect service choices.

### Key concepts
- Hair has layers, and the **cuticle** is the outer protective layer.
- The **growth cycle** explains how hair grows, rests, and sheds.
- **Scalp analysis** helps identify normal vs abnormal conditions before service.
- Know common conditions that are **safe to work around** versus conditions that require caution or refusal.
- **pH basics** matter because products can affect hair and scalp differently depending on whether they are more acidic or alkaline.
- **Porosity** is the hair’s ability to absorb moisture.
- **Elasticity** is the hair’s ability to stretch and return.
- Damaged, overly porous, or weak hair usually needs gentler handling.

### Common exam traps
- Confusing **porosity** with **elasticity**.
- Picking an aggressive service for hair that already shows **damage or weakness**.
- Treating scalp analysis like a diagnosis instead of a service-safety check.
- Forgetting that pH affects how products interact with hair.

### Quick memory cues
- **Cuticle = outside shield.**
- **Porosity = absorbs. Elasticity = stretches.**
- **Weak hair plus strong chemicals is usually a bad combo.**
- **Analyze before you apply.**

### Self-check
- What is the main difference between porosity and elasticity?
- Why does scalp analysis matter before service?
- What does the cuticle do?
- If hair is fragile and overly porous, should your service choice be stronger or gentler?

## Module 4: Shampooing, Conditioning, and Draping

### Overview
This module tests basic service procedure and safety. These questions can look simple, but they often check whether you understand sanitation, client protection, proper draping, and when not to continue with a service.

### Key concepts
- **Client preparation** starts before water or product touches the hair.
- **Draping** protects the client’s skin and clothing while helping maintain cleanliness.
- Shampoo procedure should be **orderly, gentle, and sanitary**.
- **Scalp massage** should be appropriate to the service and client condition.
- Different **conditioners** serve different purposes, such as moisture, smoothing, or treatment support.
- Recognize **contraindications**, including scalp irritation, open lesions, or signs that make a service unsafe.
- Water temperature, product choice, and clean setup all matter.

### Common exam traps
- Poor draping that leaves the client unprotected.
- Continuing with shampoo or massage when the scalp shows **irritation or broken skin**.
- Using rough handling instead of safe, controlled movements.
- Forgetting sanitation during what seems like a “basic” service.

### Quick memory cues
- **Protect first, shampoo second.**
- **Drape correctly or the whole setup is already wrong.**
- **If the scalp is irritated, slow down and reassess.**
- **Simple service does not mean careless service.**

### Self-check
- Why is proper draping important?
- What should make you stop or delay shampoo service?
- What is the purpose of conditioner in general?
- Why do exam questions on simple services still focus heavily on safety?

## Module 5: Haircutting and Hairstyling

### Overview
This module is about understanding how technique creates result. The exam usually focuses less on artistic style and more on the meaning of core terms like elevation, overdirection, tension, and guideline, plus safe use of heated tools.

### Key concepts
- **Sectioning** creates control.
- A **guideline** tells you the length to follow.
- **Elevation** changes weight and layering.
- **Overdirection** shifts where length is kept.
- **Tension** affects accuracy and consistency.
- Know the difference between **blunt cuts, graduation, uniform layers, and long layers**.
- **Thermal styling safety** matters: check tool temperature, protect hair, and avoid scalp burns.
- Blow-dryers and irons should be used with awareness of heat damage risk.

### Common exam traps
- Confusing **elevation** and **overdirection**.
- Mixing up which cut creates **weight** and which removes it.
- Forgetting that heat tools can damage hair or burn the client.
- Choosing the technique that sounds stylish instead of the one that matches the haircut result described.

### Quick memory cues
- **Guideline = your map.**
- **Elevation changes layers.**
- **Overdirection shifts length.**
- **More heat is not more skill.**

### Self-check
- What does a guideline do?
- How does elevation affect the haircut result?
- What is overdirection used for?
- What is one major safety concern with thermal styling?

## Module 6: Chemical Services

### Overview
This is a big exam category and a place where safe reasoning matters a lot. Questions often test whether you understand patch tests, strand tests, developers, color basics, and the risks of overprocessing. If a choice sounds chemically aggressive on already compromised hair, be suspicious.

### Key concepts
- **Haircolor types** vary by how long they last and how deeply they affect the hair.
- **Developers** help color processes work, and stronger is not automatically better.
- **Patch testing** helps check for possible sensitivity or allergic reaction when required.
- **Strand testing** helps predict processing results.
- **Color theory basics** help explain tone and correction.
- **Lighteners, perms, and relaxers** all require careful timing, product control, and safety awareness.
- **Overprocessing** can lead to breakage, scalp irritation, and serious hair damage.
- Chemical services should never be approached casually on damaged hair or irritated scalp.

### Common exam traps
- Confusing **patch test** with **strand test**.
- Choosing a stronger chemical option when the hair is already fragile.
- Ignoring scalp condition before starting a chemical service.
- Treating processing time like a suggestion instead of a safety limit.
- Forgetting that chemical caution beats speed.

### Quick memory cues
- **Patch test = skin reaction. Strand test = hair result.**
- **Damaged hair does not need “just one more process.”**
- **Chemicals plus irritation = stop and rethink.**
- **On chemical questions, the safest valid answer is often the best answer.**

### Self-check
- What is the difference between a patch test and a strand test?
- Why is overprocessing dangerous?
- What should you consider before applying a chemical service to the scalp area?
- If hair is weak and compromised, should you increase or reduce chemical intensity?

## Module 7: Skin Care and Nail Care Basics

### Overview
You are not being tested as a specialist in every advanced service here. The exam usually wants safe fundamentals: basic structures, infection-control habits, and when a skin or nail condition means you should not continue.

### Key concepts
- Know the **basic layers of the skin** and simple structure questions.
- **Basic facial safety** includes sanitation, clean implements, and avoiding treatment over unsafe conditions.
- **Contraindications** matter in both skin and nail services.
- Understand **nail structure** and common service-safety rules.
- **Manicure safety** includes clean tools, proper handling, and avoiding broken skin or visible infection concerns.
- Infection control rules apply just as much here as in hair services.

### Common exam traps
- Continuing a service over **inflamed, broken, or obviously infected** tissue.
- Forgetting that implements for skin and nail work still require correct cleaning and disinfection.
- Confusing basic structure terms.
- Treating a visible problem instead of recognizing it as a contraindication.

### Quick memory cues
- **If skin or nails look unsafe, do not push through.**
- **Clean hands, clean tools, clean setup.**
- **Know the structure, but respect the condition.**
- **No service is worth spreading infection.**

### Self-check
- What is a contraindication in skin or nail services?
- Why is infection control important in manicures and facials?
- What should you do if tissue is broken or visibly irritated?
- Are structure questions only about memorization, or do they also affect safe practice decisions?

## Module 8: Anatomy, Chemistry, and Test Strategy

### Overview
This module ties the science pieces together and helps you answer questions more accurately. The anatomy and chemistry parts are usually basic but important. The test strategy part matters because many wrong answers are technically tempting but less safe, less complete, or less specific.

### Key concepts
- Learn common **anatomy terms** that describe body structure and location.
- Know basic **chemistry vocabulary** used in cosmetology.
- Understand **solutions, suspensions, and emulsions** at a practical level.
- Know the difference between **acids and alkalis** in simple terms.
- Understand **oxidation-reduction basics** enough to recognize how they relate to chemical services.
- Know a little **basic electricity and electrical safety**, especially simple current concepts, common units, and safe use of salon equipment.
- For multiple-choice questions, read carefully and look for the **best** answer, not just one that sounds partly true.
- Words like **most, first, best, safest, except, and not** can completely change the answer.

### Common exam traps
- Rushing past key question words like **except** or **first**.
- Choosing an answer that is true in some situations, but not the **best** choice in the one given.
- Freezing on science vocabulary and missing the simpler idea underneath.
- Forgetting that state board questions often reward **safety, sanitation, and legality** over speed or style.

### Quick memory cues
- **Read the whole question, then answer it.**
- **Best beats barely true.**
- **Science words are easier when tied to service use.**
- **If two answers seem possible, pick the safer and more professional one.**

### Self-check
- What is the difference between a solution and an emulsion?
- Why do words like “except” and “first” matter so much on exams?
- When two answers both sound plausible, what kind of answer usually wins on state board questions?
- How do chemistry basics help with cosmetology questions?
