## Module 3: Hair and Scalp Care

### What the exam is really testing
This section is usually testing whether you can look at the hair and scalp and make a smart service decision. It is less about memorizing fancy science words and more about knowing what the words mean in practice. Can you tell the difference between normal and not-normal? Can you connect porosity, elasticity, and pH to a safer service choice? That is the real game.

### Key concepts
- **Cuticle** is the outer layer. Think of it as the hair's protective covering.
- **Cortex** gives hair much of its strength, elasticity, and natural pigment.
- **Hair growth cycle** matters because not all hair is growing at the same time.
- **Scalp analysis** is a safety and service check, not a medical diagnosis.
- **Porosity** is how easily hair absorbs and holds moisture or chemicals.
- **Elasticity** is how well hair stretches and returns without breaking.
- **pH** affects how products interact with the hair and scalp. In plain English, product chemistry can make the hair lie flatter, swell more, or react differently.
- Hair that is **overly porous, dry, weak, or damaged** usually needs gentler handling and more caution.

### Common confusions or traps
- Mixing up **porosity** and **elasticity**.
- Seeing an unhealthy scalp condition and acting like your job is to diagnose it.
- Choosing a strong service for hair that already shows signs of damage.
- Forgetting that scalp and hair analysis happen **before** the service, not halfway through when problems show up.

### Quick memory cues
- **Porosity = absorbs. Elasticity = stretches.**
- **Cuticle outside, cortex inside.**
- **Analyze first, then decide.**
- **Weak hair does not want a rough service.**

### Mini example
A client has hair that feels rough, takes product quickly, and seems fragile when stretched. That points more toward **high porosity and poor elasticity**, which means a cautious approach makes more sense than a stronger chemical or overly aggressive styling plan.

## Module 4: Shampooing, Conditioning, and Draping

### What the exam is really testing
This module is really testing whether you know how to protect the client during a basic service. The exam likes these questions because students sometimes relax too much on "simple" services. But shampooing and draping still involve sanitation, client comfort, proper setup, and noticing when something is unsafe. In other words, this module is about doing the basics correctly, not casually.

### Key concepts
- **Client preparation comes first.** Before water, before shampoo, before anything else, the client should be properly draped and positioned.
- **Draping protects skin and clothing.** It also helps keep the service sanitary and professional.
- **Shampooing should be gentle and controlled.** Rough scrubbing is not better. It is just rough.
- **Water temperature matters.** Too hot can irritate the scalp and make the service uncomfortable.
- **Product choice matters.** Different shampoos and conditioners are made for different needs, such as moisture, smoothing, clarifying, or treatment support.
- **Conditioner helps improve manageability and moisture balance.** On exam questions, think about what the conditioner is meant to do for the hair, not just that it goes on after shampoo.
- **Scalp massage should be appropriate and safe.** It should not be done over irritated, broken, or obviously unsafe areas.
- **Contraindications matter even in a shampoo service.** Open lesions, visible irritation, or other unsafe scalp conditions should make you stop and reassess.

### Common confusions or traps
- Thinking draping is just about neatness instead of protection.
- Treating shampoo as a mindless step and forgetting sanitation.
- Continuing with shampoo or scalp massage when the scalp shows **broken skin, abrasions, inflammation, or irritation**.
- Picking a harsh or random product instead of one that fits the hair and scalp condition.
- Assuming conditioner is always the same, when questions may test purpose or product matching.

### Quick memory cues
- **Protect first, shampoo second.**
- **Basic service, same safety rules.**
- **If the scalp is angry, do not keep going.**
- **Conditioner is for the hair's needs, not just the routine.**

### Mini examples
**Example 1:** A client is draped poorly and water could run onto the neck and clothing. Even if the shampoo itself is correct, the setup is already wrong. The exam often wants you to notice the protection failure first.

**Example 2:** During scalp inspection, you notice an open sore. The right move is not to "be extra gentle." The safer answer is to avoid continuing the service and respond appropriately.

**Example 3:** Hair feels dry, tangles easily, and looks dull after cleansing. A conditioner aimed at moisture and manageability makes more sense than treating all conditioners as interchangeable.

## Module 5: Haircutting and Hairstyling

### What the exam is really testing
This section is really testing whether you understand how haircutting terms create results. The exam is usually not asking whether you can invent a trendy style. It wants to know if you understand the language of haircutting: where the weight sits, how length is controlled, what a guideline does, and how elevation and overdirection change the shape. On the styling side, it also checks whether you remember that hot tools can absolutely hurt hair and scalp if used carelessly.

### Key concepts
- **Sectioning** creates clean control. Messy sectioning usually leads to messy results.
- A **guideline** is the reference point that tells you what length to follow.
- **Stationary guideline** stays in one place. **Traveling guideline** moves as you work.
- **Elevation** is the angle at which hair is held when cutting. It affects layering and weight.
- **Overdirection** means combing hair away from its natural falling position before cutting. It helps preserve length in certain areas.
- **Tension** affects precision. Uneven tension can create uneven results.
- **Blunt haircut** usually keeps more weight and gives a solid shape.
- **Graduation** builds weight and shape through stacked layers.
- **Uniform layers** create more even layering throughout.
- **Long layers** remove weight while keeping overall length more than shorter layered shapes.
- **Thermal styling safety** matters with blow-dryers, flat irons, and curling irons. Heat can damage the hair or burn the client.

### Common confusions or traps
- Mixing up **elevation** and **overdirection**.
- Forgetting that **guidelines control consistency**.
- Confusing which haircut keeps weight versus which removes it.
- Acting like more tension or more heat automatically means more control.
- Ignoring the condition of the hair before using thermal tools.

### Quick memory cues
- **Guideline = length map.**
- **Elevation changes weight and layers.**
- **Overdirection shifts where length stays.**
- **Blunt keeps weight. Layers remove weight.**
- **Hot tool safety is part of styling, not an extra.**

### Mini examples
**Example 1:** A question asks which technique helps keep length in the front while cutting from another position. That is pointing toward **overdirection**, not elevation.

**Example 2:** A haircut description says the shape is heavy and solid with little layering. That should push you toward **blunt cutting**, not uniform layers.

**Example 3:** A client has fragile, previously processed hair. The best styling answer is not the hottest possible iron setting. The exam usually rewards the safer heat choice and proper caution.

## Module 7: Skin Care and Nail Care Basics

### What the exam is really testing
This module usually tests whether you can stay safe and sanitary in services involving the skin and nails. It is not trying to turn you into a dermatologist or podiatrist. It is checking whether you know the basic structures, can recognize obvious contraindications, and understand that infection control still matters just as much here as it does in hair services.

### Key concepts
- Know the **basic skin layers** well enough to handle simple structure questions.
- In facials and skin services, **clean hands, clean implements, and clean setup** still matter every time.
- **Contraindications** include visible conditions or signs that make a service unsafe to continue.
- You may identify that something looks abnormal, inflamed, broken, or infected-looking, but that does **not** mean you diagnose it.
- Know the **basic parts of the nail**, such as the natural nail structure and surrounding tissue.
- **Cuticle area and surrounding skin should be handled carefully.** Rough handling creates injury risk.
- **Manicure and skin-service tools** must follow proper cleaning and disinfection rules.
- Broken skin changes the safety picture. Once tissue is open, infection-control concern goes up fast.

### Common confusions or traps
- Continuing a service over **broken, inflamed, swollen, or clearly infected-looking tissue**.
- Treating visible skin or nail problems instead of recognizing them as reasons to stop or avoid service.
- Forgetting that reusable implements for nails and skin still require proper disinfection.
- Mixing up simple structure terms because they all start sounding alike.
- Thinking a small amount of irritation does not matter. On the exam, it often does.

### Quick memory cues
- **If tissue looks unsafe, do not push through.**
- **Clean hands, clean tools, clean setup.**
- **Recognize, do not diagnose.**
- **Broken skin raises the stakes.**

### Mini examples
**Example 1:** A manicure question mentions redness, swelling, and broken skin around the nail. The exam is usually not asking how to work around it. It is testing whether you know the service should not continue normally.

**Example 2:** A facial setup uses an implement that was only wiped off, not properly cleaned and disinfected. The trap is thinking it looks clean enough. The safer answer is that appearance alone is not enough.

**Example 3:** A question asks about basic nail structure and then gives answer choices that sound medical. Usually the exam wants the simple cosmetology-level structure term, not the fanciest-sounding option.
