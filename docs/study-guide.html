Module 1

Infection Control and Sanitation

Overview: This is one of the most important areas on the written exam because it applies to almost every service. When a question gives you multiple possible answers, the safest sanitary answer is often the winner. This section is not about sounding technical. It is about understanding how to prevent cross-contamination and protect the client, yourself, and the salon environment.

What the exam is really testing

Most sanitation questions are trying to see whether you understand order of operations and whether you can tell the difference between what is reusable, what can be disinfected, and what has to be thrown away. They also test whether you stay calm and do the correct procedure when blood or contamination is involved.

Key concepts

  • Cleaning removes visible dirt, debris, and residue. It does not mean the item is ready to use on the next client.
  • Disinfection is used on nonporous tools and surfaces after cleaning.
  • Sterilization is a higher level than disinfection, but it is not the default answer for most everyday salon questions unless the exam clearly points there.
  • Porous items absorb material and usually cannot be safely disinfected once contaminated.
  • Nonporous items can often be cleaned and then disinfected properly.
  • Single-use items should be thrown away immediately after use.
  • Universal precautions mean treating blood and body fluids as potentially infectious.
  • Handwashing and gloves are basic habits, not optional extras.

How to reason through the common question types

If the question mentions visible dirt, residue, or buildup, think clean first. If the item is nonporous and reusable, then think about disinfection. If the item is porous, absorbent, broken, or single-use, the safest answer is usually discard it. If blood is involved, the tone of the question changes immediately. Slow down and follow blood exposure logic instead of choosing the fastest answer.

Mini examples

Example: A used cotton pad touched a client’s skin and product. Can it be disinfected and reused? No. It is disposable and should be thrown away.

Example: A metal tool has visible debris on it. Is disinfectant alone enough? No. Clean first, then disinfect.

Common exam traps

  • Mixing up cleaning and disinfection.
  • Choosing to disinfect something that is porous or single-use instead of discarding it.
  • Forgetting that blood exposure questions require a more careful response than normal cleanup.
  • Picking the answer that sounds fastest instead of the one that is most sanitary.

Quick memory cues

  • See dirt? Clean first.
  • Hard surface = maybe disinfect. Soft/porous = probably toss.
  • Blood changes everything.
  • Safe beats fast.

Module 2

California Law and Professional Safety

Overview: This module is about choosing the legal, safe, and professional action. On the written exam, you will often see answers that sound friendly or helpful but are still wrong because they ignore safety, scope of practice, or contraindications.

What the exam is really testing

These questions are really asking whether you know your lane. Can you recognize when a service should be refused, stopped, delayed, or referred out? Can you stay professional without pretending to be a medical provider? Can you keep safety above convenience?

Key concepts

  • Scope of practice means only performing services your license allows.
  • A license should be current, valid, and properly displayed as required.
  • Contraindications are signs or conditions that make a service unsafe or inappropriate.
  • Refusing service can be the correct professional action when safety is at risk.
  • You may recognize visible issues, but you should not act like a doctor or diagnose disease.

How to reason through the common question types

If an answer choice asks you to continue a service on irritated skin, a contagious condition, broken skin, or something clearly outside your license scope, that answer is usually dead on arrival. The exam tends to reward the choice that protects the client, follows professional boundaries, and stays inside legal scope.

Mini examples

Example: A client shows signs of a contagious condition in the service area. The right answer is not to “be gentle and continue.” The right answer is to stop or refuse the service and respond appropriately.

Example: A client asks you to explain what medical condition they have. The correct move is not to diagnose. It is to stay inside your role and refer them appropriately if needed.

Common exam traps

  • Choosing a helpful-sounding answer that is outside legal scope.
  • Confusing good customer service with doing whatever the client wants.
  • Continuing a service because the condition seems “minor” even though the question clearly signals a contraindication.
  • Forgetting that professionalism includes saying no when no is the safe answer.

Quick memory cues

  • If it is unsafe, illegal, or out of scope, do not do it.
  • Nice is good. Safe is required.
  • You are licensed to perform services, not diagnose disease.

Module 3

Hair and Scalp Care

Overview: This section covers the basic science behind hair services. You do not need deep chemistry here. You do need to understand the structure of hair, common scalp analysis ideas, and why porosity, elasticity, and pH affect service choices.

What the exam is really testing

This section is usually testing whether you can look at the hair and scalp and make a smart service decision. It is less about memorizing fancy science words and more about knowing what the words mean in practice. Can you tell the difference between normal and not-normal? Can you connect porosity, elasticity, and pH to a safer service choice? That is the real game.

Key concepts

  • Cuticle is the outer layer. Think of it as the hair's protective covering.
  • Cortex gives hair much of its strength, elasticity, and natural pigment.
  • Hair growth cycle matters because not all hair is growing at the same time.
  • Scalp analysis is a safety and service check, not a medical diagnosis.
  • Porosity is how easily hair absorbs and holds moisture or chemicals.
  • Elasticity is how well hair stretches and returns without breaking.
  • pH affects how products interact with the hair and scalp.

Common confusions or traps

  • Mixing up porosity and elasticity.
  • Seeing an unhealthy scalp condition and acting like your job is to diagnose it.
  • Choosing a strong service for hair that already shows signs of damage.
  • Forgetting that scalp and hair analysis happen before the service.

Quick memory cues

  • Porosity = absorbs. Elasticity = stretches.
  • Cuticle outside, cortex inside.
  • Analyze first, then decide.
  • Weak hair does not want a rough service.

Mini example

A client has hair that feels rough, takes product quickly, and seems fragile when stretched. That points more toward high porosity and poor elasticity, which means a cautious approach makes more sense than a stronger chemical or overly aggressive styling plan.

Module 4

Shampooing, Conditioning, and Draping

Overview: This module is really testing whether you know how to protect the client during a basic service. The exam likes these questions because students sometimes relax too much on “simple” services. But shampooing and draping still involve sanitation, client comfort, proper setup, and noticing when something is unsafe.

What the exam is really testing

The basics. Client protection, proper draping, gentle technique, product matching, and whether you stop when the scalp condition says stop. This is not glamorous, but it is part of being safe and competent.

Shampoo product types — know these

The exam expects you to match product to need. Memorize these four:

  • Clarifying shampoo: Removes product buildup, hard water minerals, and excess oil. Use when hair feels heavy or dull. Not for daily use — it strips too aggressively.
  • Moisturizing / conditioning shampoo: For dry, damaged, or chemically treated hair. Adds hydration while cleansing gently.
  • Balancing shampoo: For oily scalps. Removes excess sebum without over-drying the hair lengths.
  • Color-safe / sulfate-free shampoo: For color-treated hair. Mild enough to preserve color deposit and keep the cuticle smooth.

Draping order matters

For a basic wet service: towel first around the neck and shoulders, then the cape on top. The towel absorbs water; the cape protects clothing.

For a chemical service: towel goes on the client's skin first, then the cape goes over the towel. This keeps chemical products off the skin at the neckline. The order flips because the priority shifts from water control to chemical protection.

Key concepts

  • Client preparation comes first. Before water or shampoo, the client should be properly draped and positioned.
  • Shampooing should be gentle and controlled. Rough scrubbing irritates the scalp and is not more effective.
  • Water temperature matters. Too hot irritates the scalp; too cold is uncomfortable. Warm and consistent is correct.
  • After shampooing, blot and squeeze — do not rub. Rubbing with a towel roughs up the cuticle and causes breakage.
  • Scalp massage uses the pads of the fingers, never the nails. Scratching or pressing too hard can break the scalp.
  • Conditioner is applied to the hair, not the scalp, unless a scalp treatment product is specified.
  • Contraindications matter even in a shampoo service. Broken skin, open sores, and inflamed scalps are all reasons to stop or modify.

Common confusions or traps

  • Thinking draping is just about neatness instead of protection.
  • Mixing up the draping order for wet vs. chemical services.
  • Continuing with shampoo or scalp massage when the scalp shows broken skin, abrasions, inflammation, or irritation.
  • Picking a moisturizing shampoo for an oily scalp, or a clarifying shampoo for color-treated hair.
  • Applying conditioner to the scalp when it should go on the mid-lengths and ends only.

Quick memory cues

  • Protect first, shampoo second.
  • Chemical service: towel under cape. Wet service: towel under cape, but for water not chemicals.
  • If the scalp is angry, do not keep going.
  • Blot, do not rub. Pads of fingers, not nails.
  • Match the shampoo to the scalp condition, not just the hair type.

Mini examples

Example 1: A client is draped poorly and water runs onto the neck and clothing. Even if the shampoo itself is correct, the setup is already wrong.

Example 2: During scalp inspection, you notice an open sore. The safer answer is not “be extra gentle.” It is to avoid continuing the service normally.

Example 3: A client has color-treated hair and an oily scalp. The best product choice is a color-safe shampoo, not a clarifying shampoo that would strip the color.

Module 5

Haircutting and Hairstyling

Overview: This section is really testing whether you understand how haircutting terms create results. The exam is usually not asking whether you can invent a trendy style. It wants to know if you understand the language of haircutting, where the weight sits, how length is controlled, what a guideline does, and how elevation and overdirection change shape.

What the exam is really testing

Vocabulary with consequences. If you understand what the core haircutting terms actually do, the questions become much easier. On the styling side, it also checks whether you remember that hot tools can damage hair and scalp if used carelessly.

Key concepts

  • Sectioning creates clean control.
  • A guideline is the reference point that tells you what length to follow.
  • Stationary guideline stays in one place. Traveling guideline moves as you work.
  • Elevation affects layering and weight.
  • Overdirection helps preserve length in certain areas.
  • Tension affects precision.
  • Blunt haircut usually keeps more weight.
  • Graduation builds weight through stacked layers.
  • Uniform layers create more even layering throughout.
  • Thermal styling safety matters with blow-dryers, flat irons, and curling irons.

Common confusions or traps

  • Mixing up elevation and overdirection.
  • Forgetting that guidelines control consistency.
  • Confusing which haircut keeps weight versus which removes it.
  • Acting like more tension or more heat automatically means more control.

Quick memory cues

  • Guideline = length map.
  • Elevation changes weight and layers.
  • Overdirection shifts where length stays.
  • Blunt keeps weight. Layers remove weight.
  • Hot tool safety is part of styling, not an extra.

Mini examples

Example 1: A question asks which technique helps keep length in the front while cutting from another position. That is pointing toward overdirection.

Example 2: A haircut description says the shape is heavy and solid with little layering. That should push you toward blunt cutting.

Module 6

Chemical Services

Overview: This is another high-value exam category. It combines technical knowledge with safety judgment. Questions often test whether you understand patch tests, strand tests, developers, lighteners, perms, relaxers, and overprocessing risk. If a choice sounds too aggressive for already compromised hair, be suspicious.

What the exam is really testing

Chemical-service questions are not just about memorizing product terms. They are often testing whether you can choose the safer plan, recognize warning signs, and avoid making damage worse. This is one of the clearest places where the safest reasonable answer often beats the fastest or most dramatic one.

Key concepts

  • Patch testing checks for possible sensitivity when required.
  • Strand testing predicts how the hair is likely to respond.
  • Developers help color processes work, but stronger is not automatically better.
  • Color theory explains tone and neutralization.
  • Lighteners, perms, and relaxers all require timing, control, and caution.
  • Overprocessing can lead to breakage, irritation, and serious damage.

How to reason through the common question types

If the hair is already damaged, overly porous, breaking, or the scalp is irritated, the safest answer is rarely the strongest formula or the most aggressive next step. Slow down. Reassess. Chemical questions often reward caution. If you see a choice that sounds like “just push through,” it is usually the trap.

Mini examples

Example: A client reports burning during a chemical service. The answer is not to ignore it and wait. The correct response is to stop and assess safely.

Example: Hair is already weak and previously processed. Is stronger chemical action the right choice? Usually no. Reduced intensity or a safer plan makes more sense.

Common exam traps

  • Confusing patch test with strand test.
  • Choosing a stronger chemical option when the hair is already fragile.
  • Ignoring scalp condition before starting a chemical service.
  • Treating processing time like a suggestion instead of a safety limit.

Quick memory cues

  • Patch test = skin reaction. Strand test = hair result.
  • Damaged hair does not need “just one more process.”
  • Chemicals plus irritation = stop and rethink.
  • On chemical questions, the safest valid answer is often the best answer.

Module 7

Skin Care and Nail Care Basics

Overview: This module tests whether you know basic skin and nail structure, can recognize contraindications, and understand that sanitation rules apply here just as much as anywhere else. You are not expected to diagnose — but you are expected to know when to stop.

Skin structure — the layers you need to know

The skin has two main layers the exam tests on:

  • Epidermis: The outermost layer. This is what you see and touch. It is made mostly of dead keratinized cells that shed and regenerate. It contains no blood vessels. The bottom of the epidermis (the basal layer) is where new cells are produced.
  • Dermis: The deeper layer beneath the epidermis. This is where the real structural work happens — it contains collagen fibers, elastin, blood vessels, nerve endings, hair follicles, sebaceous (oil) glands, and sweat glands.

Melanocytes are found in the epidermis and produce melanin, the pigment that gives skin and hair their color.

Sebaceous glands produce sebum, the skin's natural oil, which lubricates and protects the skin surface. Overactive sebaceous glands contribute to oily skin.

Keratinocytes produce keratin, the same structural protein found in hair and nails.

Nail structure — the parts you need to know

  • Nail matrix: The living tissue under the base of the nail where new nail cells are produced. Damage to the matrix can permanently affect nail growth.
  • Nail plate: The hard, visible part of the nail. It is made of keratinized cells.
  • Nail bed: The skin directly beneath the nail plate. The nail plate rests on the nail bed.
  • Free edge: The part of the nail that extends past the fingertip — the part you file and shape.
  • Lunula: The white, half-moon shape visible at the base of the nail plate. It is the visible portion of the nail matrix.
  • Eponychium (cuticle): The overlapping skin at the base of the nail plate. It seals the space between the nail and the skin to prevent infection.
  • Hyponychium: The skin under the free edge where the nail plate separates from the nail bed.

Contraindications — when to stop

Both skin and nail services have contraindications. The exam tests whether you recognize them and respond correctly.

  • Fungal nail infection: Thickened, discolored, brittle nails. Do not perform a manicure — service could spread infection or worsen it. Refer to a physician.
  • Broken or inflamed skin: Open wounds, active inflammation, or infection anywhere in the service area. Stop the service.
  • Highly inflamed skin conditions: Pustules, active rashes, or conditions that appear infected on the face during a facial. Refer out, do not proceed.
  • Suspicious lesions or growths: Anything that looks abnormal, raised, discolored, or unusual. Recognize it — do not diagnose it — and refer the client to a doctor.

What the exam is really testing

Whether you know the structure terms well enough to answer straightforward questions, and whether your default response to unsafe-looking tissue is to stop and refer — not to work around it carefully.

Common confusions or traps

  • Mixing up epidermis vs. dermis — epidermis is outer, no blood vessels; dermis is deeper, has blood vessels and glands.
  • Mixing up lunula vs. matrix — the lunula is the visible white crescent; the matrix is the tissue producing the nail, mostly hidden beneath the skin.
  • Mixing up eponychium vs. hyponychium — eponychium is at the base (cuticle); hyponychium is under the free edge.
  • Continuing a service over contraindicated tissue instead of stopping.
  • Forgetting that reusable nail implements still require full disinfection between clients.

Quick memory cues

  • Epidermis = outer, no blood. Dermis = deeper, has everything.
  • Melanocytes make melanin. Sebaceous glands make sebum.
  • Matrix makes the nail. Lunula is what you can see of it.
  • Cuticle (eponychium) = base. Hyponychium = under the free edge.
  • Recognize, do not diagnose. If it looks wrong, stop.

Mini examples

Example 1: A question describes a nail that is thickened, yellow, and brittle. The answer is not "proceed carefully." The service should not continue — this is a contraindication.

Example 2: A question asks which skin layer contains collagen and blood vessels. That is the dermis, not the epidermis.

Example 3: A client has redness and pus around the nail fold during a manicure. Stop the service and refer. This is not a sanitation question — it is a contraindication recognition question.

Module 8

Anatomy, Chemistry, and Test Strategy

Overview: This module covers the science vocabulary behind cosmetology services and the test-taking strategy that separates students who know the material from students who pass the exam. Both matter. You can know a lot and still lose points by reading too fast or picking an answer that is true but not the best one.

Basic body anatomy — what the exam tests

The exam is not a biology exam. You need enough anatomy to answer straightforward structure questions, not to understand every system in depth.

  • Anatomy is the study of body structures — what the parts are and where they are.
  • Physiology is the study of how those structures function.
  • The skin is the largest organ of the body. It protects, regulates temperature, and is the primary surface cosmetologists work on.
  • Keratin is the fibrous protein that makes up hair, nails, and the outer skin layer. Most cosmetology services involve changing or protecting keratin.
  • Bones and muscles come up lightly. Know that the skull, facial bones, and neck muscles affect positioning during services. The trapezius and sternocleidomastoid are the neck muscles most referenced.

pH — the scale that explains most chemical services

pH stands for potential of hydrogen. It measures how acidic or alkaline a substance is on a scale from 0 to 14.

  • 0–6: Acidic. The lower the number, the more acidic.
  • 7: Neutral. Pure water is pH 7.
  • 8–14: Alkaline (basic). The higher the number, the more alkaline.

Why this matters for cosmetology:

  • Hair and skin have a natural pH of around 4.5 to 5.5 — slightly acidic.
  • Acid-balanced products (shampoos, conditioners, rinses) close the cuticle and maintain the hair's natural state.
  • Alkaline products (relaxers, perms, color) open the cuticle to allow the chemical to enter the cortex. This is necessary but controlled — too alkaline for too long causes damage.
  • After a chemical service, an acidic neutralizer or normalizing shampoo is used to close the cuticle and restore pH balance.

Chemistry vocabulary — the terms that show up on the exam

  • Solution: A mixture where one substance is completely dissolved in another (e.g., salt dissolved in water). Uniform throughout.
  • Suspension: A mixture where particles are dispersed but not dissolved. They settle over time (e.g., calamine lotion — shake before use).
  • Emulsion: A mixture of two substances that do not naturally mix, held together by an emulsifier (e.g., oil and water in a conditioner). Creams and lotions are emulsions.
  • Surfactant: The active ingredient in shampoo. It has one end attracted to water and one end attracted to oil, which is how it lifts oil and debris from the hair so water can rinse it away.
  • Buffer: An ingredient added to products to stabilize pH so it does not shift during use.

Oxidation-reduction (redox) — why it matters in chemical services

Most major chemical services involve a redox reaction. Understanding this at a basic level makes chemical service questions much easier.

  • Oxidation: A substance loses hydrogen or gains oxygen. In hair color, the developer (hydrogen peroxide) oxidizes the color molecules, causing them to expand inside the cortex and become trapped.
  • Reduction: A substance gains hydrogen or loses oxygen. In a perm, the reducing agent (ammonium thioglycolate) breaks the disulfide bonds in the cortex so the hair can be reshaped.
  • The two reactions work together: reduction breaks bonds, neutralization re-forms them in the new shape.
  • The exam may ask which step "softens" the hair (reduction/waving lotion) vs. which step "hardens" or "fixes" the shape (oxidation/neutralizer).

Electricity basics — the supporting topic

The exam tests basic electrical safety, not electrical engineering. You need to know enough to answer one or two questions.

  • Conductors are materials that electricity passes through easily — metals, water, and the human body are conductors. This is why wet hands and electrical tools are dangerous.
  • Insulators block electricity — rubber, glass, and dry wood are insulators. Tool handles and cords use insulating materials for safety.
  • Grounding gives electrical current a safe path to the earth if there is a fault, preventing shock.
  • GFCI outlets (ground fault circuit interrupters) are required near water sources in salons. They cut power instantly if they detect a fault — this is a key safety feature the exam references.
  • Never use electrical tools near water, on wet hair at the scalp, or with a damaged cord.

Test strategy — how to read questions correctly

The California written exam uses multiple-choice questions where more than one answer can seem reasonable. Strategy matters as much as knowledge.

  • Watch for qualifier words: "best," "first," "most," "safest," "except," and "not" change the entire question. Circle or underline these when you see them.
  • Best beats barely true: If two answers are both technically correct, the one that is safest, most professional, or most complete is usually right.
  • Eliminate the obvious wrong answers first: Most questions have at least one or two clearly wrong options. Cross those off and decide between the remaining ones.
  • Safety and professionalism usually win: On judgment questions, the answer that prioritizes client safety and follows professional protocol is almost always correct.
  • Do not overthink recall questions: If you know the term, trust it. Overthinking leads to second-guessing correct answers.
  • If you are stuck, pick the most cautious answer: The exam reflects the Board's standards — when in doubt, the more conservative, safer choice aligns with what they want.

Common confusions or traps

  • Mixing up oxidation vs. reduction — oxidation adds oxygen or removes hydrogen; reduction does the opposite. In perms, the waving lotion is the reducing step.
  • Confusing solution, suspension, and emulsion — solutions are fully dissolved, suspensions settle, emulsions are oil-water mixtures.
  • Thinking pH 7 is always the target — the hair's natural pH is 4.5–5.5, which is slightly acidic, not neutral.
  • Missing qualifier words and answering a different question than the one asked.
  • Picking an answer that sounds smart rather than the one that is safest.

Quick memory cues

  • pH under 7 = acid (closes cuticle). Over 7 = alkaline (opens cuticle).
  • Hair's natural pH is 4.5–5.5 — slightly acidic.
  • Reduction breaks bonds (waving lotion). Oxidation re-forms them (neutralizer).
  • Surfactant = lifts oil so water can rinse it.
  • GFCI near water. Grounding = safety path. Wet hands + electricity = stop.
  • Best, first, safest — read the qualifier before you answer.
  • If two answers seem right, pick the safer one.

Mini examples

Example 1: A question asks which step in a perm service softens and breaks the hair's bonds. That is the reducing step — the waving lotion. The neutralizer re-forms the bonds.

Example 2: A question asks what pH range is considered the hair's natural state. The answer is 4.5–5.5, not 7. pH 7 is neutral water, not hair.

Example 3: A question asks "which of the following is the BEST first response if a client reports scalp burning during a chemical service?" Two answers mention stopping the service. The better one is "rinse immediately and assess the scalp" — it adds the action after stopping. Best beats vague.
